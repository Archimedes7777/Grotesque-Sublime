# Prompt library: healthcare technology management and medical device education

**Niche rank:** 3 of 5 (score 67). See `01-niche-research.md` for the evidence.

**Thesis:** there is no consistent, credible educational channel of size for biomedical equipment technicians, clinical engineers, nurses who fight with equipment, and people deciding whether to enter the field. The audience is small compared with finance or AI, but it has institutional budgets (hospitals, vendors, training programs), a certification to prepare for, and almost no competition. This is the moat niche: the owner's CBET credential and 8 years of practice cannot be copied by a faceless automation channel.

**Why it ranks below niches 1 and 2:** the view ceiling. Ad revenue alone will be modest. Revenue comes from B2B sponsors (test equipment, CMMS software, training providers), exam preparation products, and consulting. Health is also a "sensitive" category for the AI-content label.

**Main risks:** employer policy and confidentiality; never show patient data or a specific hospital's devices and procedures without permission; regulatory accuracy (FDA, TJC, CMS, NFPA 99, AAMI) must be cited and dated.

## Context block (paste before every prompt)

```text
Context for every answer:
- Project name: [working title]. Channel handle: not created yet.
- What the channel is about: how medical equipment actually works, fails, and gets fixed, and how the healthcare technology management field runs: device fundamentals, troubleshooting method, preventive maintenance logic, electrical safety, regulatory context, and career paths into and through the field, with AI tools used to speed the work.
- Why me: CBET-certified biomedical equipment technician with about 8 years in healthcare technology management, plus AI and prompt engineering experience. I can explain devices from the physics up and the compliance down, and I use AI tooling in the job.
- Target viewer: three groups. (1) Working BMETs and clinical engineers, ages 22 to 55, mostly US, who search for troubleshooting, PM procedures, and CBET exam preparation. (2) Nurses, respiratory therapists, and technologists who want to understand the devices they use. (3) Career changers and students who search "how to become a biomedical equipment technician", "BMET salary", "CBET exam".
- Formats available: whiteboard or slides with voiceover (primary), bench demonstrations on owner-controlled or decommissioned equipment, screen recording for CMMS and AI workflows, talking head optional, Shorts cut from long-form.
- Assets on hand: 8 years of field experience, CBET exam knowledge, access to public standards summaries and FDA databases (MAUDE, recalls), a repository of AI-agent security policy, and AI workflow skills for PM report and procedure writing.
- Time budget: [hours] per week for research, production, and posting, by one person.
- Goal for the first 90 days: 30 long-form videos, Partner Program thresholds in progress, a CBET study guide as a lead magnet, 1 conversation with a test-equipment or CMMS vendor.
- Constraints: no patient data, no identifiable hospital systems, no current employer procedures or contracts, no manufacturer service-manual pages reproduced beyond fair use, no repair instructions for life-support devices that a viewer could follow unsafely without training; regulatory statements cited to the primary source and dated; medical claims avoided entirely.
- Rules: every post is a draft a person reviews and publishes by hand; no automated likes, follows, comments, DMs, or uploads; no tool receives passwords, cookies, or tokens; AI-generated or altered content gets the platform's label where its rules require it (health is a sensitive category, so expect the prominent label if a synthetic voice is used).
- Mark [Unverified] any platform threshold, policy, standard revision, or price you cannot confirm, with the date of your knowledge.
```

## Niche-specific notes for all prompts

- **Category positioning:** Education & Science, not Health & Sport (measured median RPM $10.22 versus $1.23). Titles and descriptions should read as engineering education, not medical advice.
- **Safety language:** every bench demonstration carries an on-screen note that device service requires manufacturer training and institutional authorization. Use blockquote-style warnings in scripts.
- **Sponsor fit:** electrical safety analyzers and simulators, CMMS platforms, parts suppliers, training and certification providers, staffing firms. Flat fees are common in B2B; CPM pricing matters less.
- **AI angle:** the owner's AI skills are a differentiator inside the niche (PM procedure drafting, report summarization, parts lookup), and they link this channel to niche 1.

## Prompts in recommended order

### Step 1. Niche evaluation

```text
Act as a YouTube market researcher and channel strategist.
Using the context above, evaluate "healthcare technology management and medical device education" as a niche. Cover: audience demand and search intent for each of the three viewer groups (top 20 search phrases), competition (name every channel you can find that covers BMET, clinical engineering, or device troubleshooting consistently, and what each lacks), monetization ceiling by revenue type (ads, B2B sponsors, exam prep products, consulting), content sustainability over 2 years, and the angle the owner's CBET credential plus AI skills gives.
Define the ideal viewer for each group in one paragraph each, recommend 3 to 4 content categories, and give 30 video ideas ranked by expected pull, each tagged Short or long-form, with the device or topic, the viewer group, and the safety or confidentiality constraint it must respect.
Flag ideas that would require footage of equipment the owner does not control, and replace them with whiteboard or simulator versions.
```

### Step 2. Channel plan

```text
Act as a YouTube strategist.
Using the context above, write a 12-week plan with:
- Positioning statement (one sentence) and a channel description under 1,000 characters that signals engineering education, not medical advice.
- Content pillars with a target share: device fundamentals (how it works, from physics to front panel), troubleshooting method and case walkthroughs (anonymized), standards and compliance explained (NFPA 99, AAMI, TJC, CMS, FDA), career and CBET preparation, and AI tools for the job.
- Formats and lengths: which pillars are 8 to 15 minute long-form, which are Shorts.
- A publishing cadence that fits the time budget, with hours assigned to research, recording, editing, and posting.
- Retention tactics for technical education: the failure symptom first, the mechanism second, the fix third, the "what the standard says" last.
- A weekly action checklist the owner follows by hand.
Leave monetization out; it is a separate prompt for a later phase.
```

### Step 3. Scripts, hooks, titles, thumbnails

```text
Act as a YouTube content strategist and editor for a technical education channel.
Using the context above, take the top 10 ideas from Step 1 and produce for each:
- 3 title options under 60 characters: specific, name the device or standard and the question answered, no "shocking", no all-caps.
- 1 thumbnail concept: the device or a schematic fragment, one highlighted component, an overlay of 3 words or fewer.
- The first 15 seconds as a script: the symptom or the question a tech or nurse actually asks, then the promise of the mechanism and the fix.
- A section outline with the mechanism explained before the midpoint, the case or demonstration after it, the standard or regulatory note, and a safety statement at the end.
- One line on why a viewer stays to the end.
Add a blockquote safety warning to any script that shows service steps.
Plain, concrete language. No em dashes, no "it's not X, it's Y" reveals, no engagement bait.
```

### Step 4. Analytics loop

```text
Act as a YouTube data analyst.
I will paste numbers I export by hand from YouTube Studio (impressions, click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed, returning viewers, subscribers gained, traffic sources, top videos, viewer geography). You do not have account access and must not ask for it.
Build:
1. A weekly review template that takes 10 minutes: what to record, and what counts as signal versus noise at low view counts, given that this niche's total audience is small.
2. A monthly review template: which viewer group, device category, and format worked, what to repeat, what to stop.
3. Decision rules, for example: low click-through rate with high average view duration means change the thumbnail or title, not the video; strong career-content views with weak device-content views means lead with career and bridge to devices.
4. The three metrics that matter most in the first 90 days for a small-audience professional niche and why the others can wait.
State the sample sizes below which a conclusion is premature.
```

### Step 5. Growth mechanics

```text
Act as a YouTube growth strategist with expertise in content discovery.
Using the context above, explain how YouTube surfaces niche professional education (search intent, suggested videos next to vendor demos and training snippets, browse, the Shorts feed) in plain terms, focused on what a one-person channel can act on.
Then give a practical growth plan: upload frequency within the time budget, how to test a title or thumbnail with few views, how to rank for career and exam searches that have steady demand, how to reach nurses and technologists who do not search for BMET terms, which Shorts to cut from each long-form video, and how to turn a viewer into a subscriber without begging.
Rules: every engagement action is manual by the owner. No automation, follow-for-follow, engagement pods, or paid views. No outreach to hospitals or vendors that could be read as soliciting on an employer's behalf. Mark platform mechanics you cannot verify as [Unverified] with a date.
```

### Step 6. Production workflow

```text
Act as a production manager for a one-person technical education channel.
Using the context above, design a repeatable weekly workflow from idea to published video and Shorts cut from the same material. Cover: topic selection, primary-source research (FDA databases, standards summaries, manufacturer public documentation), script with cited claims, whiteboard or slide production, bench recording on owner-controlled or decommissioned equipment, voiceover, editing, thumbnail, metadata, the AI-content disclosure step (expect the prominent health label if a synthetic voice is used), and the human review and publish step.
Constraints: the publish step is always a person in YouTube Studio; do not propose auto-upload tools. Any tool you recommend must be named with its cost [Unverified] and must not need account passwords or session cookies. Include a quality checklist (no patient or employer data, no identifiable hospital assets, citations dated, safety warning present, no life-support service steps a viewer could follow unsafely, sponsor disclosure, AI label) and estimated minutes per step so the total fits the time budget.
```

### Step 7. Monetization sequence (run after 30 videos or 90 days of data)

```text
Act as a creator business strategist specializing in professional and B2B education channels.
Using the context above and the analytics so far, lay out the revenue options in the order they become realistic: YouTube Partner Program (state current thresholds and mark them [Unverified] with a date), B2B sponsorships from test-equipment, CMMS, parts, and training vendors (flat-fee and CPM models, disclosure wording, and conflicts to avoid with an employer), a CBET exam preparation product, paid procedure and checklist packs, consulting to hospitals and vendors, memberships, newsletter, and a course only if viewers ask.
For each give requirements, pros, limits, and the trigger that says "now is the time".
Rules: sponsorships never change a technical recommendation and are disclosed on screen and in the description; no product that could be mistaken for manufacturer-authorized service training; check employer conflict-of-interest policy before any paid relationship.
```
